\begin{abstract}
Generative models for garment design are evaluated on whether they emit a
\emph{well-formed} sewing pattern, not on whether the pattern can be cut and
sewn. We measure the gap on GarmentCodeData~\citep{gcdata}, the de facto
pre-training corpus for the field. Across 3{,}450 published patterns --- which
already passed the authors' own checks and survived physical simulation ---
20.4\% contain a defect that makes the garment unmanufacturable, including
edges as short as $0.018$\,cm and corners of $0.16^\circ$. A further 69.1\%
cannot be judged at all, because the seam format records \emph{which} edges are
joined but not \emph{why} their lengths differ, leaving an intentional gather
indistinguishable from a defect. We contribute three minimal format extensions
that make this information expressible, and an open-source validator covering
panel geometry, seam topology and geometric wearability that is deterministic,
CPU-only and runs in about 10\,ms per pattern.

Two results bear directly on how such a validator should be used. First, the
obvious application --- discarding every pattern that reports an error --- is
counterproductive: it retains 10.5\% of the corpus and halves the share of
full-body garments, because errors accumulate with seam count while the
\emph{per-seam} error rate is flat. Filtering on hard geometric defects alone
retains 79.6\% and leaves the composition intact. Second, a paired comparison
over 2{,}953 designs rendered on both a neutral and a randomized body finds no
effect of body shape on defect rate (McNemar $p = 0.91$): the defects belong to
the parametric design, not to the made-to-measure fit, and are therefore
reproducible and fixable at source.

Applying the same measurement to a published generative model, none of 200
patterns produced by AIpparel is manufacturable. The model emits patterns of the
same complexity as the corpus --- 31.6 seams per pattern against 31.5 --- while
88\% of its seams join edges of unequal length: it does not sew less, it sews
worse.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A render is not a garment. Current generative fashion tools produce images, and
turning an image into something a factory can cut still requires a human
patternmaker. A parallel line of work therefore targets the sewing pattern
itself: the 2D panels, their boundary curves, and the seams that join
them~\citep{garmentcode,chatgarment,aipparel,sewingldm}.

These systems are evaluated, implicitly or explicitly, on whether their output
\emph{parses}: panels close, the seam graph resolves, the generator returns
without raising. We call this property \emph{validity}. The property industry
needs is different --- whether the pattern can be cut from fabric and sewn by a
machine --- and we call it \emph{manufacturability}. Validity does not imply it.

The gap is not a technicality. An edge measuring $0.018$\,cm cannot be cut. A
corner of $0.16^\circ$ cannot be sewn without the fabric bunching. Two edges
sewn together whose lengths differ by $9\%$ will pucker --- unless the
difference is a gather the designer asked for, in which case it is correct
construction. Nothing in the current representation distinguishes these two
cases, and so no validator built on it can either.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item A corpus-scale measurement of manufacturability on GarmentCodeData:
  20.4\% of published patterns carry a hard geometric defect and only 10.5\%
  are clean, on patterns that already survived the authors' pipeline including
  physical simulation (Section~\ref{sec:res-gap}).
  \item Three minimal format extensions --- for seam ease, edge finishing, and
  seam orientation --- that make recoverable the intent the current
  representation discards (Section~\ref{sec:intent}); the third turns out not
  to be inferable from the file at all.
  \item An open-source validator for panel geometry, seam topology and
  geometric wearability, including a purely 2D criterion for tangent continuity
  across seam crossings that needs no 3D assembly (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A negative result with a practical remedy: the natural way to use such
  a validator for corpus sanitization is biased against complex garments, and a
  defect-class-aware filter avoids it (Section~\ref{sec:res-filter}).
  \item A null result: made-to-measure fitting to randomized bodies does not
  degrade pattern geometry (Section~\ref{sec:res-body}), which localizes the
  defects to the parametric design program.
  \item The metric applied to a released generative model: none of 200 patterns
  from AIpparel is manufacturable, and the failure is localized to seam length
  agreement rather than to garment complexity or to the conditioning modality
  (Section~\ref{sec:res-gen}).
  \item A calibration account, with three independent instances, arguing that
  suppressing legitimate construction is the harder half of the problem
  (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Pattern representation.}
GarmentCode~\citep{garmentcode} expresses garments as parametric programs over
roughly 80 parameters, emitting a JSON pattern in which each panel carries 2D
vertices, edges with optional cubic Bézier curvature, a 3D placement, and a
semantic label such as \code{arm} or \code{torso}. Each seam is a pair of
references $\{\textit{panel}, \textit{edge}\}$. GarmentCodeData~\citep{gcdata}
scales this to roughly 115{,}000 synthetic garments and is the de facto
pre-training corpus for the models below.

\paragraph{Generative models.}
ChatGarment~\citep{chatgarment} has a vision--language model emit GarmentCode's
parameter schema key by key; its expressive ceiling is therefore exactly
GarmentCode's space. AIpparel~\citep{aipparel} instead tokenizes geometry
directly as drawing commands (\code{MOVE}, \code{LINE}, \code{CURVE},
\code{CUBIC}, \code{ARC}) with seams encoded as labels shared between edges.
This breaks the parametric ceiling but offers no validity guarantee; its own
code defines decoding error types for malformed sequences. Because seams are
labels rather than constraints, nothing ties the two edges they join to a common
length --- a gap we measure in Section~\ref{sec:res-gen}.

\paragraph{What existing systems check.}
GarmentCode performs two checks: pairwise self-intersection of edges within a
panel, and total garment length against body height. It does not check sewable
radii, cuttable detail size, fabric roll width, seam length agreement, or
wearability. To our knowledge no published system checks these, and no
benchmark reports manufacturability as a metric.

\paragraph{Analogy to program repair.}
Our framing follows the compiler loop familiar from code-generating models: a
cheap, deterministic checker returns localized, structured errors, and the
generator edits the specific site rather than regenerating. The contribution
here is less the loop than the checker it presupposes, which for sewing
patterns did not exist.

\section{A Manufacturability Validator}
\label{sec:method}

We organize checks into levels ordered by cost, so each filters before the next
spends compute. Level~0 covers the geometry of a single panel; Level~1 the seam
graph; Level~2 wearability. Everything reported here is pure geometry:
deterministic, CPU-only, no dependency on the generator that produced the
pattern.

\subsection{Levels 0 and 1}

Level~0 checks that each panel's boundary is a closed cycle of edges, that
every edge is long enough to cut and every curve wide enough to sew, that
corners are not too tight, that no two edges of a panel cross away from a
shared vertex, and that the panel fits the fabric roll. Level~1 checks the seam
graph: that references resolve, that each seam joins exactly two edges, that no
edge is sewn twice, that the garment is connected, and that the two sides of
each seam agree in length or declare why they do not.

\subsection{Tangent continuity without 3D assembly}
\label{sec:continuity}

A neckline is assembled from edges belonging to different panels and should
read as one smooth curve on the finished garment. Checking this requires no 3D
assembly. Where a seam ends, the two panels lie on either side of it and the
garment's free boundary passes from the neighbouring edge of one panel to the
neighbouring edge of the other. The angle the boundary turns through is the sum
of the two panels' interior angles at that corner, so continuity is
\begin{equation}
  \alpha + \beta \approx 180^\circ,
  \label{eq:cont}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha$ and $\beta$ each measured in its own panel's 2D frame. A
deviation means the assembled outline has a corner where a curve was presumably
intended. We report it as a warning, not an error: a kinked neckline is
perfectly sewable, so this is design quality rather than manufacturability.

\subsection{Wearability from free-boundary loops}
\label{sec:wear}

The edges that are not sewn form closed loops on the assembled garment --- the
neckline, the hem, the cuffs --- and the girth of each is the sum of its edge
lengths, computed exactly. Compared against a body measurement, this answers
whether the head passes through the neckline without simulating anything. The
check is gated on the pattern declaring what each opening must clear, how much
the fabric stretches there, and whether a closure opens it
(Section~\ref{sec:intent}); without those, girths are reported but nothing is
judged.

\subsection{Assembly order}

The natural question --- does a feasible sewing order exist? --- cannot
discriminate, because for a connected seam graph one always does. The
informative quantity is how much of the garment must be sewn in the round:
every seam joining two panels already connected by another path closes a tube,
which is slower and needs a different machine setup. Which seams these are
depends on the order chosen; how many there are does not, being the cyclomatic
number $E - V + C$ of the seam graph. True feasibility is a question of access
--- whether the needle reaches the seam line when it must be sewn --- which
requires the garment in 3D. We report cost, not impossibility.

\section{Declaring Intent}
\label{sec:intent}

A seam recorded as $\{\textit{panel}, \textit{edge}\}$ states which edges meet
and nothing else. When the two sides differ in length, the information needed
to judge the difference --- was it wanted? --- was never written down. This is a
representation defect, not a validator defect: no checker operating on this
format can recover it. We propose three minimal extensions.

\paragraph{Seam ease.} An optional \code{ease} field on a seam declares a
gather, an eased seam, or a stretch fabric, with the expected length ratio and
a tolerance:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=]
{"panel": "skirt_f", "edge": 2,
 "ease": {"type": "gather", "ratio": 1.4,
          "tol": 0.05}}
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The resulting rule is not ``this is forbidden'' but ``this must be declared'',
which gives three outcomes rather than two: declared and consistent with the
geometry is valid; declared and contradicted by it is an error; undeclared is
an error. We verify all three directions in the test suite.

\paragraph{Edge finishing.} An edge sewn to nothing may be a defect or may
carry a hem, facing, binding or opening. We add a \code{finish} declaration on
the edge itself, since a free edge appears in no seam record. Declaring a
finish on an edge that \emph{is} sewn is a contradiction and reported as an
error.

Unlike a seam mismatch, an undeclared finish is only a warning. The distinction
constrains this whole family of checks and is worth stating: a seam mismatch has
a geometric trigger --- the two lengths disagree --- so the error fires on
evidence. A free edge has no such trigger; every free edge is simply free.
Treating the absence of a declaration as an error would invalidate 100\% of
existing patterns unconditionally, which is the failure mode of
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}. A declaration requirement can carry error
severity only where geometry can contradict it.

\paragraph{Seam orientation.}
\label{par:orient}
The format records that edge $e$ of panel $P$ is sewn to edge $f$ of panel $Q$,
but not which endpoint of $e$ meets which endpoint of $f$. Any check that
reasons about what happens \emph{at the ends} of a seam needs this, and we find
it is not recoverable from the file. We tested five criteria, and report the
negative result because it motivates the extension:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item The stored 3D placement is the simulator's initial configuration and
  leaves panels roughly 60\,cm apart.
  \item Sewn edges are mostly straight or symmetric, so their shape does not
  fix the traversal direction.
  \item The assembled free boundary closes under either convention.
  \item Assembled corner chains are consistent under either convention.
  \item Interior corner angles do not sum to $360^\circ$ even under the correct
  convention, because a godet skirt is conical by design.
\end{enumerate}

We therefore add an \code{orient} declaration (\code{direct} or
\code{reversed}). It is the only one of our three extensions that cannot be
checked against geometry --- which is precisely why it must be declared --- but
it can be checked against topology: a free edge can only continue into another
free edge, so the orientation matching more free neighbours across the seam is
the physically consistent one. On the reference t-shirt \code{reversed} matches
at 12 crossings and \code{direct} at 4, and a declaration contradicted this way
is reported as a warning.

The consequence is quantitative, not cosmetic. Under the wrong orientation the
four openings of a t-shirt are recovered as two loops of $129.6$\,cm, so every
girth computed from them is meaningless. Where orientation is undeclared, our
continuity findings are evaluated under both pairings and credited with the
more favourable one, making them a lower bound; each finding records whether
its pairing was declared or inferred.

\section{Calibration Is Half the Problem}
\label{sec:calibration}

Our first working validator rejected 100\% of patterns. The same failure
recurred twice more, unprompted, as we added checks. We report all three
because the literature on generative patterns reports none, and because the
recurrence suggests the problem is structural rather than accidental:
legitimate construction reliably looks defective to a geometric rule stated
without its exception.

\paragraph{Dart tips read as defective corners.} 143 of the reported sharp
corners were dart tips: two straight edges of identical length meeting at a
point, which is ordinary construction in any skirt or trouser. Recognizing that
signature --- straightness plus length symmetry within tolerance --- removes
them.

\paragraph{Crossings counted at the shared vertex.} Two edges meeting at a
shared vertex trivially intersect there. Our tolerance was expressed in the
curve's parameter domain rather than in distance, so the trivial crossing
survived. Measured in centimetres, the false positive disappears.

\paragraph{Godet hems read as kinks.} The continuity check of
Section~\ref{sec:continuity} flagged 19 crossings on a sound godet skirt: a
triangular godet inserted into a skirt turns the hemline by roughly
$51^\circ$ where it enters, by design. The discriminating signature is that a
corner between two \emph{straight} edges is drawn, not accumulated --- a
neckline is traced with curves and is meant to flow, whereas a godet hem turns
between two straight lines because that is what was designed. The separation is
clean: in the reference t-shirt all six straight-to-straight crossings deviate
by exactly $0.00^\circ$, while in the godet skirt all 26 crossings are
straight-to-straight with a median deviation of $49^\circ$. Suppressing them
leaves the skirt silent and the t-shirt with two genuine warnings of
$29.2^\circ$ at the armhole, symmetric between left and right.

A checker is only as useful as its precision. One that rejects everything
conveys no information, cannot gate a pipeline, and cannot serve as a reward
signal. We consider the suppressed cases results on equal footing with the
retained ones.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Corpus.} GarmentCodeData v2 is distributed as 395\,GB of batches,
each packing its garments into a single archive, so pattern specifications
cannot be requested individually. We streamed one batch's archive and retained
only the specification files: 5.12\,GB transferred, 96\,MB written. This yields
3{,}450 patterns rendered on the neutral body and 3{,}307 on randomized bodies,
of which 2{,}953 designs appear in both.

These are \emph{published} patterns: they passed GarmentCode's own checks and
survived physical simulation in the authors' pipeline. The sample is therefore
cleaner than one drawn by sampling the parameter space directly, where we
measured only 30\% of 60 random designs producing a pattern the generator
itself accepts.

\paragraph{Generated patterns.} To apply the same measurement to a generator we
use AIpparel~\citep{aipparel}, whose public checkpoint emits pattern geometry
directly --- vertices and stitches rather than design parameters --- so its
outputs enter the validator without an intermediate synthesis step. We sample
100 garments from the corpus batch above and query the model twice for each: once
from the front render the corpus publishes, and once from a description derived
from that garment's own design parameters. The pairing is deliberate. All three
groups describe the same 100 garments, so the comparison isolates the
conditioning modality rather than the draw of garments, and the corpus patterns
themselves serve as a ground-truth column measured by the same validator ---
without which a rate of zero would not distinguish a failing model from an
unreachable bar. Inference ran on a single 16\,GB GPU at roughly 10\,s per
sample. The released checkpoint is stored in fp32 while the model instantiates
in bf16, so loading it with \code{torch.load} materializes 25\,GiB of anonymous
memory; memory-mapping the file instead keeps the resident set under 1.5\,GiB
and makes the run fit.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} All measurements run on CPU with fixed thresholds;
the validator, its 40-case test suite, the sweep tool and the scripts that
generate the figures in this paper from the corpus are released together, as
are the four scripts behind the generator experiment.

\paragraph{A note on the dataset's license.} GarmentCodeData states no license
in its distribution, its documentation, or its project page, and its repository
record was unreachable at the time of measurement. We therefore report
statistics and do not redistribute any subset. The dataset requires
citation~\citep{gcdata}, which we give.

\subsection{Naive filtering collapses the corpus}
\label{sec:res-filter}

The obvious application of a validator to training data is to keep the patterns
that pass and discard the rest. On this corpus that retains 362 of 3{,}450
patterns, and the survivors are systematically simpler: a median of 6 panels
and 14 seams against 10 and 30 for the rejected. The share of curved edges is
identical at 25\% in both groups, so the selection is on size, not on shape.

The cause is not that complex garments are worse made. Figure~\ref{fig:accum}
plots the defect-free rate against seam count together with the rate predicted
if defects were independent across seams. The \emph{per-seam} error rate is
flat and if anything declines with garment size, from $0.264$ in patterns under
ten seams to $0.177$ above fifty-five. A binary per-pattern filter therefore
selects against size by construction, because a garment with thirty
opportunities to fail usually takes one.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{acumulacion.pdf}
\caption{Defect-free rate by seam count (log scale), against the rate expected
if defects were independent across seams. The observed curve lies orders of
magnitude above the prediction --- at 20--30 seams, 8.5\% against 0.4\% --- so
defects cluster within patterns: a genuine per-pattern quality signal exists.
But the decline with size is an accumulation artefact, not a quality gradient.}
\label{fig:accum}
\end{figure}

The gap between the two curves is itself informative. If defects were
independent, a thirty-seam garment at $0.209$ errors per seam would be clean
$0.4\%$ of the time; it is clean $8.5\%$ of the time, twenty times more often.
Some patterns are systematically good and others systematically bad, so a
per-pattern quality signal is real --- the binary filter simply confounds it
with size.

Figure~\ref{fig:filters} compares filtering criteria by what they do to the
garment-type composition of the corpus. Discarding only hard geometric defects
retains 79.6\% of the data and leaves the composition nearly intact (full-body
garments 60.3\%\,$\rightarrow$\,58.2\%), whereas the zero-error filter retains
10.5\% and halves the full-body share to 31.5\%. The reason is the same one
that made the 89.5\% headline misleading: undeclared mismatches are format
ambiguity, not defects, and using them as a discard criterion is what destroys
the corpus.

\caption{Garment-type composition of the corpus retained by each filtering

\subsection{Body shape does not affect defect rates}
\label{sec:res-body}

GarmentCodeData renders each design on a neutral body and on randomized bodies.
A plausible hypothesis is that made-to-measure fitting degenerates at the
extremes of the body distribution, narrowing panels until they are uncuttable.
It does not. Aggregates are nearly identical: 20.4\% against 21.2\% of patterns
with a hard defect, and $0.195$ against $0.198$ errors per seam.

Aggregates can hide an effect when the two groups contain different designs, so
we compare \emph{paired} over the 2{,}953 designs present in both, which holds
the design fixed and varies only the body. Of these, 2{,}248 are clean on both
and 424 defective on both; the discordant cases split 139 (defective only on
the randomized body) against 142 (only on the neutral body). An exact McNemar
test gives $p = 0.91$: no asymmetry of any size.

The accompanying positive result is the useful one. Of the 705 designs
defective on at least one body, 424 are defective on both --- 60\% concordance,
which would not arise if the body were the cause. \textbf{The defects belong to
the parametric design, not to the fit}, which makes them reproducible and
fixable at source, and means corpus sanitization need not weight by body type.

\subsection{Manufacturability of generated patterns}
\label{sec:res-gen}

The corpus measurement bounds what a model trained on it can learn. The
complementary question is what a model actually emits. Table~\ref{tab:generated}
reports AIpparel on the 100 paired garments of Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
& Manufacturable & Seams & Open seams \\
\midrule
AIpparel, from image & \textbf{0 / 100} & 31.6 & 88\% \\
AIpparel, from text  & \textbf{0 / 100} & 26.8 & 86\% \\
Corpus (ground truth) & 12 / 100 & 31.5 & 33\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Manufacturability of generated patterns against the corpus patterns
they were conditioned on. ``Seams'' is the mean seam count per pattern;
``open seams'' the fraction joining edges of unequal length with no declared
gather.}
\label{tab:generated}
\end{table}

None of the 200 generated patterns is manufacturable. With zero successes in
200 trials the 95\% upper confidence bound is 1.5\%, so the rate is low
regardless of how the run happened to fall.

The headline is less informative than the mechanism. A count of errors per
pattern rewards whichever model emits fewer seams, so it cannot be compared
across groups; the per-seam rate can. AIpparel produces patterns of
\emph{the same complexity} as the corpus --- 31.6 seams against 31.5 --- and
fails at 88\% of them, against 33\% for the corpus. The model does not sew less;
it sews worse. This is legible in the architecture: the two edges of a seam are
emitted as independent vertex predictions and nothing in the representation
couples their lengths. That a pattern \emph{depicts} the requested garment and
that its seams \emph{close} are distinct objectives, and only the first is
trained.

The six-point gap between image conditioning (23\% free of hard defects) and
text conditioning (17\%) does not survive the paired test it invites. Of the 100
garments, 17 are clean only from the image and 11 only from the text; an exact
McNemar test on the 28 discordant cases gives $p = 0.345$. On this evidence the
conditioning modality does not change manufacturability, which is consistent
with the failure being a property of the output head rather than of how well the
garment was understood.

Two cautions attach to this number. The descriptions are synthetic, derived from
design parameters rather than written by a person; were they worse than natural
language, that would penalize the text condition, which is precisely the one
that showed no difference. More importantly, AIpparel was trained on
GarmentCodeData and these garments are drawn from it, so if they appeared in its
training set, 0\% is the benign case rather than the adversarial one.

\subsection{Runtime}

Validation costs about $10$\,ms per pattern on one CPU core (11.0\,ms and
8.3\,ms on our two reference garments), so the full 115{,}000-garment corpus is
roughly twenty core-minutes. This matters for the intended uses: a filter that
costs as much as what it filters is not a filter. Reaching that figure required
one optimization --- self-intersection testing was 96\% of runtime, from
re-linearizing the second edge inside the first edge's loop and from comparing
all edge pairs irrespective of proximity. Linearizing once per edge and
rejecting pairs by bounding box is exact, not approximate: boxes are dilated by
the vertex tolerance before comparison, so the filter can only do redundant
work, never miss a crossing.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The validator's most valuable use is upstream.} The obvious
application is the repair loop: return localized findings to the generator and
let it edit the offending site, as a code model does with compiler diagnostics.
Our findings carry the panel, edge or seam reference and the measured values
for exactly this. But the larger effect is on training data. A corpus of
115{,}000 garments with this defect profile teaches those defects as correct
construction, and Section~\ref{sec:res-filter} shows that cleaning it requires
care: the naive filter trades a visible bias for an invisible one.

\paragraph{Reporting manufacturability as a metric.} The fraction of
manufacturable outputs is comparable, reproducible and GPU-free, and the field
currently does not report it. We suggest it belongs alongside the geometric
reconstruction metrics these systems already publish. Section~\ref{sec:res-gen}
shows why the two do not substitute for each other: a model can place panels
that reconstruct the target garment well and still leave nearly every seam
unclosable, because no reconstruction metric is sensitive to whether two edges
that must be sewn together have the same length. Reporting the per-seam rate
alongside the per-pattern fraction is worth the extra line, since the first is
comparable across models of different output complexity and the second is not.

\paragraph{Format over checker.} Three of our contributions are format
extensions rather than checks, and we think that ordering reflects the problem.
Two thirds of the patterns in Table~\ref{tab:breakdown} fail for want of a
field, not for want of a validator.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Thresholds are uncalibrated.} The default limits are physically
plausible but have not been contrasted against real fabric by a professional
patternmaker. They are an explicit hypothesis. The relative claim --- that
published patterns contain defects nobody checks for --- does not depend on the
exact values, and Table~\ref{tab:margins} shows the margins are wide, but the
absolute counts should be read as threshold-conditional.

\paragraph{One batch of twenty-four.} We measured both body variants of a
single batch. Batch-to-batch variation is unmeasured, though the two body
variants agreeing so closely is mild evidence for homogeneity.

\paragraph{No downstream demonstration.} We describe the validator's use as a
filter, a repair signal and a training reward, and we characterize how
filtering should be done, but we do not report a trained model that uses it.
Establishing that a correctly filtered corpus improves a downstream generator
is the natural next experiment and is not claimed here.

\paragraph{Coverage and inference.} Assembly checking reports the cost of
sewing in the round rather than true feasibility, which is a question of
physical access and requires 3D. Drape, tension maps and dynamic poses are
outside this work entirely; we deliberately do not report simulated verdicts,
since without measured fabric parameters they would assert something unverified.
Finally, continuity findings are a lower bound wherever \code{orient} is
undeclared, which on existing corpora is everywhere.

\paragraph{One generator measured, not a comparison.} The corpus measurements
are on GarmentCode output and the generative measurement is on a single AIpparel
checkpoint over 100 garments, so Table~\ref{tab:generated} is a measurement, not
a ranking. Extending it to ChatGarment is not symmetric work: that model emits
design parameters rather than geometry, so its outputs must pass through
GarmentCode before they can be validated, and the resulting number would
characterize the model-plus-synthesizer pair rather than the model. That
asymmetry has to be declared for the comparison to mean anything. Our remaining
claims about the expressive ceilings of these architectures are structural,
drawn from reading their code.

\section{Conclusion}

Generative garment systems are evaluated on a property that does not imply the
one industry needs. Measured on the field's own pre-training corpus, one in
five published patterns cannot be manufactured, and two thirds more cannot be
judged because the format does not record whether a seam mismatch was wanted.
We release a deterministic, CPU-only validator, three format extensions that
make that intent expressible, and the benchmarks behind these numbers.

The same measurement applied to a released model returns zero manufacturable
patterns in 200, at the same seam complexity as the corpus it was trained on.
The defect is specific --- edges that must be sewn together are predicted
independently and do not end up the same length --- which is the kind of failure
a metric can drive out and a reconstruction loss cannot see.

Two findings should change how such a tool is used. Filtering a training corpus
on ``no errors'' is worse than not filtering, because it selects for small
garments rather than good ones; filtering on hard geometric defects retains
four times the data and leaves the distribution intact. And the defects are a
property of the design program rather than of made-to-measure fitting, which
means they are reproducible, attributable, and fixable where they are
introduced.
