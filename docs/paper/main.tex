\title{\bfseries Valid Is Not Manufacturable:\\
A Measurement Study of Generative Sewing Patterns\\
and a Validator for What Compilers Miss}

\begin{abstract}
Generative models for garment design are evaluated on whether they produce a
\emph{well-formed} sewing pattern, not on whether the resulting pattern can be
cut and sewn. We show that the two are far apart. Auditing 30 patterns that
GarmentCode~\citep{garmentcode} accepts as valid, we find manufacturability
defects in 23 of them (77\%): edges as short as $0.07$\,cm, corners of $9^\circ$,
curvature radii of $0.13$\,cm, and seams whose two sides differ in length with
no stated reason. Sampling the parameter space at random, only 30\% of 60
designs yield a pattern the generator itself accepts, which contradicts the
common assumption that a parametric system is valid by construction. We further
identify a representation-level cause for a large share of these defects: the
seam format records \emph{which} edges are joined but not \emph{why} they
differ, so a $9\%$ length mismatch is indistinguishable from an intentional
gather. Implementing the checks surfaced two further omissions of the same
kind --- how a free edge is finished, and which endpoint of a seam meets which
--- neither of which is recoverable from the file. We propose three minimal
format extensions that make this information expressible, and release an
open-source validator implementing two levels of checks --- panel geometry and
seam topology --- that is deterministic, runs in milliseconds on CPU, and emits
structured findings suitable for automated repair, dataset filtering, and use
as a training reward. We also report a reproducible bug in
GarmentCode's own self-intersection test, and argue from our own experience
that calibrating such a validator against false positives is as much of the
problem as detecting defects: our first version rejected 100\% of patterns,
almost entirely by reading legitimate dart tips as defective corners.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A render is not a garment. Current generative fashion tools produce images,
and turning an image into something a factory can cut requires a human
patternmaker. A parallel line of work therefore targets the sewing pattern
itself: the 2D panels, their boundary curves, and the seams that join
them~\citep{garmentcode,chatgarment,aipparel,sewingldm}.

These systems are evaluated, implicitly or explicitly, on whether their output
\emph{parses}: whether panels close, whether the seam graph resolves, whether
the generator returns without raising. We call this property validity. The
property that matters industrially is different: whether the pattern can be cut
from fabric and sewn by a machine. We call this manufacturability, and it is
not implied by validity.

The gap is not a technicality. An edge measuring $0.07$\,cm cannot be cut. A
corner of $9^\circ$ cannot be sewn without the fabric bunching. Two edges sewn
together that differ by $9\%$ in length will either pucker or leave the garment
distorted --- unless the difference is a gather the designer asked for, in
which case it is correct construction. Nothing in the current representation
distinguishes these two cases, and so no validator built on it can either.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
  \item A measurement of how far validity is from manufacturability in a widely
  used open generative pattern system: 77\% of accepted patterns carry at least
  one defect, and only 30\% of randomly sampled parameter settings produce an
  accepted pattern at all (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).
  \item The identification of a representation-level cause --- the seam format
  does not encode intent --- quantified at 35\% of seams, and three minimal
  format extensions that close it, for seam ease, edge finishing and seam
  orientation (Section~\ref{sec:intent}).
  \item An open-source validator covering panel geometry and seam topology,
  CPU-only and dependency-light, emitting machine-readable findings for
  automated repair (Section~\ref{sec:validator}), including a purely 2D
  criterion for tangent continuity across seam crossings.
  \item A reproducible defect in GarmentCode's own self-intersection check
  (Section~\ref{sec:bug}), and a calibration account, with two independent
  instances, arguing that false positives are the harder half of the problem
  (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Pattern representation.}
GarmentCode~\citep{garmentcode} expresses garments as parametric programs over
roughly 80 parameters, emitting a JSON pattern in which each panel carries 2D
vertices, edges with optional cubic Bézier curvature, a 3D placement, and a
semantic label such as \texttt{arm} or \texttt{torso}. Each seam is a pair of
references $\{\textit{panel}, \textit{edge}\}$. GarmentCodeData~\citep{gcdata}
scales this to roughly 115{,}000 synthetic garments and is the de facto
pre-training corpus for the models below.

\paragraph{Generative models.}
ChatGarment~\citep{chatgarment} has a vision--language model emit GarmentCode's
parameter schema key by key. Its expressive ceiling is therefore exactly
GarmentCode's space: it cannot produce anything outside it.
AIpparel~\citep{aipparel} instead tokenizes geometry directly as drawing
commands (\texttt{MOVE}, \texttt{LINE}, \texttt{CURVE}, \texttt{CUBIC},
\texttt{ARC}) with seams encoded as labels shared between edges, capped at 108.
This breaks the parametric ceiling but offers no validity guarantee; its own
code defines decoding error types for malformed sequences.

\paragraph{What existing systems validate.}
GarmentCode performs two checks: pairwise self-intersection of edges within a
panel, and total garment length against body height. It does not check sewable
radii, cuttable detail size, fabric roll width, seam length agreement, or
wearability. To our knowledge no published system checks these, and no
benchmark reports manufacturability as a metric.

\paragraph{Analogy to program repair.}
Our framing follows the compiler loop familiar from code-generating models: a
cheap, deterministic checker returns localized, structured errors, and the
generator edits the specific site rather than regenerating from scratch. The
contribution of this paper is less the loop than the checker it presupposes,
which for sewing patterns did not exist.

\section{The Manufacturability Gap}
\label{sec:gap}

All measurements below were produced on CPU in a Linux container, with fixed
random seeds; the benchmark scripts are released with the validator.

\subsection{Parametric does not mean valid}

We sampled 60 designs uniformly at random over GarmentCode's parameter space
and recorded whether the generator produced a pattern it accepts.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Outcome & Cases \\
\midrule
Accepted & 18 \\
Self-intersection & 22 \\
Error (mostly length exceeded) & 20 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Outcomes over 60 random parameter settings. Only 30\% are accepted.}
\label{tab:sampling}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:sampling} contradicts the intuition that a parametric generator
is valid by construction: the parameter space is largely composed of invalid
regions, and the reference generator retries until it lands in a valid one.
Any model that emits points in this space inherits the problem.

\subsection{Seam length disagreement}

We measured the lengths of both sides of every seam across 25 accepted
patterns. Table~\ref{tab:mismatch} reports the fraction of seams exceeding a
given relative mismatch, with gathers enabled and disabled.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Threshold & With gathers & Without \\
\midrule
Mismatch $>1\%$ & 35.4\% & 23.6\% \\
Mismatch $>5\%$ & 27.8\% & 15.9\% \\
Maximum observed & 53.1\% & 52.2\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Relative seam length mismatch over 25 accepted patterns.}
\label{tab:mismatch}
\end{table}

Disabling gathers lowers the mismatch but does not remove it: gathers account
for roughly one third. The remainder comes from ease that is likewise never
declared, and the maximum barely moves. The conclusion is structural rather
than numerical --- the format has no place to record whether a mismatch is
wanted.

\subsection{Auditing accepted patterns}

Running our validator over 30 patterns that GarmentCode accepts yields
Table~\ref{tab:audit}. 23 of the 30 (77\%) carry at least one error.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrl}
\toprule
Finding & Cases & Severity \\
\midrule
Probable undeclared gather & 258 & warning \\
Undeclared seam mismatch & 111 & error \\
Edge below cuttable length & 51 & error \\
Corner too sharp & 15 & error \\
Self-intersection & 2 & error \\
Excessive curvature & 2 & error \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Findings over 30 patterns GarmentCode accepts as valid.}
\label{tab:audit}
\end{table}

The shortest edge measured $0.07$\,cm and the tightest curvature radius
$0.13$\,cm --- numerical residue rather than design intent. The sharp corners
are $9^\circ$ flaps on sleeves and collars. The two self-intersections are
cases GarmentCode's own check should have caught; Section~\ref{sec:bug}
explains why it did not.

\paragraph{Implication for training data.} These defects are not rare events in
a tail: they are present in the majority of accepted outputs. A model
pre-trained on a corpus generated this way learns them as correct construction.
We argue that filtering or score-weighting such a corpus is a prerequisite to
training, not a later refinement.

\section{Declaring Intent}
\label{sec:intent}

A seam recorded as $\{\textit{panel}, \textit{edge}\}$ states which edges meet
and nothing else. When the two sides differ in length, the information needed
to judge the difference --- was it wanted? --- was never written down. This is a
representation defect, not a validator defect: no checker operating on this
format can recover it.

We extend the seam record with an optional \texttt{ease} field:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=,caption={Declared intent on one side of a seam.}]
{"panel": "skirt_f", "edge": 2,
 "ease": {"type": "gather", "ratio": 1.4,
          "tol": 0.05}}
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
where \texttt{type} is one of \texttt{gather}, \texttt{ease}, or
\texttt{stretch}; \texttt{ratio} is how many times longer this edge is than its
counterpart; and \texttt{tol} is the relative tolerance on that ratio.

The resulting rule is not ``this is forbidden'' but ``this must be declared'',
which gives three outcomes rather than two. Declared and consistent with the
geometry: valid. Declared but contradicted by the geometry: reported as
\texttt{ease\_incongruente}. Undeclared: reported as
\texttt{desajuste\_no\_declarado}. We verify all three directions in the test
suite --- an 8\% mismatch is an error undeclared, becomes valid when declared
with the correct ratio, and returns to being an error when the declared ratio
does not match.

\subsection{Two further gaps of the same kind}

Implementing the remaining checks surfaced two more places where the format
withholds something no validator can recover.

\paragraph{Edge finishing.} An edge that is not sewn to anything may be a
defect or may carry a hem, facing, or binding. We add a \texttt{finish}
declaration on the edge itself (\texttt{hem}, \texttt{facing},
\texttt{binding}, \texttt{opening}, \texttt{raw}), since a free edge appears in
no seam record. Declaring a finish on an edge that \emph{is} sewn is a
contradiction and reported as an error.

Unlike a seam mismatch, however, an undeclared finish is reported only as a
warning. The distinction is worth stating because it constrains this whole
family of checks: a seam mismatch has a geometric trigger --- the two lengths
disagree --- so the error fires on evidence. A free edge has no such trigger;
every free edge is simply free. Treating the absence of a declaration as an
error would therefore invalidate 100\% of existing patterns unconditionally,
which is the precise failure mode of Section~\ref{sec:calibration}. A
declaration requirement can only carry error severity where geometry can
contradict it.

\paragraph{Seam orientation.}
\label{par:orient}
The format records that edge $e$ of panel $P$ is sewn to edge $f$ of panel $Q$,
but not which endpoint of $e$ meets which endpoint of $f$. This is needed by
any check that reasons about what happens \emph{at the ends} of a seam, and we
found it is not recoverable: the 3D placement stored in the file is the
simulator's initial configuration and leaves panels roughly 60\,cm apart, the
sewn edges are mostly straight or symmetric so their shape does not disambiguate
the traversal direction, and the assembled boundary closes consistently under
either convention.

We therefore add an \texttt{orient} declaration (\texttt{direct} or
\texttt{reversed}) on the seam. It is the only one of our three extensions that
cannot be checked against geometry --- which is exactly why it must be declared
--- but it can be checked against topology. A free edge can only continue into
another free edge, so the orientation that matches more free neighbours across
the seam is the physically consistent one. On the reference t-shirt,
\texttt{reversed} matches at 12 crossings and \texttt{direct} at 4; a
declaration contradicted this way is reported as a warning.

\section{A Validator for Levels 0 and 1}
\label{sec:validator}

We organize manufacturability checks into levels ordered by cost, so that each
filters before the next spends compute. Level 0 covers the geometry of a single
panel and Level 1 the seam graph; both are pure geometry, deterministic, and
run in milliseconds. Level 2 (drape, tension, wearability) requires physical
simulation and is out of scope here, as are production nesting (Level 3) and
expert review (Level 4).

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Code & Detects \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\emph{Level 0 --- panel geometry}}\\
\texttt{vertice\_inexistente} & edge references missing vertex \\
\texttt{contorno\_abierto} & edges do not form a closed cycle \\
\texttt{vertice\_huerfano} & vertex used by no edge \\
\texttt{borde\_degenerado} & edge below cuttable length \\
\texttt{curvatura\_excesiva} & radius below sewable minimum \\
\texttt{esquina\_aguda} & angle too tight to sew \\
\texttt{auto\_interseccion} & edges cross away from a vertex \\
\texttt{excede\_ancho\_rollo} & panel exceeds fabric roll width \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\emph{Level 1 --- seam graph}}\\
\texttt{costura\_no\_binaria} & seam not joining exactly two edges \\
\texttt{costura\_nula} & both edges of zero length \\
\texttt{desajuste\_no\_declarado} & 1--15\% mismatch, undeclared \\
\texttt{fruncido\_no\_declarado} & $>15\%$ mismatch (warning) \\
\texttt{ease\_incongruente} & declared ratio contradicts geometry \\
\texttt{borde\_multicosido} & edge appearing in several seams \\
\texttt{panel\_suelto} & panel sewn to nothing \\
\texttt{prenda\_desconectada} & disjoint panel groups (warning) \\
\texttt{quiebre\_en\_cruce} & boundary kinks across a seam (warning) \\
\texttt{esquina\_de\_diseno} & kink between two straight edges (info) \\
\texttt{acabado\_no\_declarado} & free edges with no finish stated (warning) \\
\texttt{acabado\_incongruente} & finish declared on a sewn edge \\
\texttt{orientacion\_dudosa} & orientation contradicts free boundary (warning) \\
\texttt{costuras\_en\_redondo} & seams that must close a tube (info) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Implemented checks. Reference-integrity codes omitted for space.}
\label{tab:checks}
\end{table*}

\subsection{Continuity across seam crossings}

A neckline is assembled from edges belonging to different panels, and should
read as one smooth curve on the finished garment. Checking this does not
require assembling anything in 3D. Where a seam ends, the two panels lie on
either side of it, and the garment's free boundary passes from the neighbouring
edge of one panel to the neighbouring edge of the other. The angle the boundary
turns through is simply the sum of the two panels' interior angles at that
corner, so continuity is
\begin{equation}
  \alpha + \beta \approx 180^\circ ,
\end{equation}
with $\alpha$ and $\beta$ measured in each panel's own 2D frame. A deviation
means the assembled outline has a corner where the design presumably intended a
curve. We report it as a warning rather than an error: a kinked neckline is
perfectly sewable, so this is a question of design quality, not manufacturability.

Because the endpoint correspondence is not stored
(Section~\ref{par:orient}), an undeclared seam is evaluated under both
pairings and credited with the more favourable one, so findings are a lower
bound; each finding records whether its pairing was declared or inferred.

\subsection{Assembly order}

The plan we started from asked whether a feasible sewing order exists. For a
connected seam graph one always does, so the question as posed cannot
discriminate. The informative quantity is instead how much of the garment must
be sewn in the round: every seam joining two panels already connected by
another path closes a tube, which is slower and needs a different machine
setup. Which seams these are depends on the order chosen; how many there are
does not, being the cyclomatic number $E - V + C$ of the seam graph. The
reference t-shirt requires 9 of its 16 seams to be closed in the round.

True assembly feasibility is a question of access --- whether the needle can
reach the seam line at the moment it must be sewn --- which requires the garment
in 3D and belongs to Level 2. We report cost, not impossibility, and say so.

\subsection{Structured output for repair}

Every finding carries a code, a severity, an exact reference to the offending
panel, edge, or seam, and the measured values:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=,caption={A finding, as emitted for a model.}]
{"nivel": 1,
 "codigo": "desajuste_no_declarado",
 "severidad": "error",
 "costura": 5,
 "medido": {"largo_a_cm": 19.88,
            "largo_b_cm": 21.90,
            "desajuste_rel": 0.0921}}
\end{lstlisting}

The model is told that seam 5 joins edges of $19.88$ and $21.90$\,cm, not that
its output was rejected. This localization is what makes repair cheaper than
regeneration, and it is the same property that makes compiler diagnostics
usable by code-generating models. We note that its usefulness depends on the
representation: a model emitting parameters can only respond indirectly, by
moving a parameter and hoping for the intended geometric effect, whereas a
model emitting geometry can move the vertex the finding names.

\subsection{Soft rules}

Thresholds live in a configurable \texttt{Limites} object because they depend
on material and workshop, and because haute couture violates several on
purpose. A system that cannot distinguish ``broken'' from ``deliberately
unconventional'' is not usable by designers, and that judgment belongs to the
designer rather than the checker. Our current implementation supports global
threshold adjustment and declared seam intent, but not yet per-finding
exceptions --- the mechanism that would let a designer accept one $9^\circ$
corner without disabling the check everywhere.

\section{A Defect in GarmentCode's Self-Intersection Check}
\label{sec:bug}

The reference implementation reduces a candidate intersection as follows:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
if t2 < t1:
    t1, t2 = t2, t1
if close_enough(t1, 0) and close_enough(t2, 1):
    intersect_t[i] = None
\end{lstlisting}

Here \texttt{t1} and \texttt{t2} are curve parameters belonging to \emph{two
different curves}, so ordering them against each other is not meaningful: the
comparison relates coordinates in unrelated parameter domains. Combined with a
low-resolution linearization of arcs, the test discards real crossings. The two
self-intersections in Table~\ref{tab:audit} were confirmed independently by
linearizing to 48 segments.

We linearize at 48 segments rather than computing arc intersections exactly
because the exact arc intersection routine in \texttt{svgpathtools} is
unreliable~\citep{svgpathtools121}. This is a limitation we inherit, not a
design choice: the check is sound up to that resolution.

\section{Calibration Is Half the Problem}
\label{sec:calibration}

Our first working validator rejected 100\% of patterns. Reviewing the findings
case by case surfaced two errors of our own, both instructive.

\paragraph{Darts read as defects.} 143 of the reported sharp corners were dart
tips: two straight edges of identical length meeting at a point, which is
ordinary construction in any skirt or trouser. Recognizing that signature ---
straightness plus length symmetry within tolerance --- reduces the count to the
15 genuine cases in Table~\ref{tab:audit}. Without the exception, every skirt
and every pair of trousers is flagged.

\paragraph{Crossings counted at the shared vertex.} Two edges meeting at a
shared vertex trivially intersect there. Our tolerance was expressed in the
curve's parameter domain rather than in distance, so the trivial crossing
survived. Measured in centimeters, the false positive disappears.

\paragraph{Godet hems read as kinks.} The continuity check of
Section~\ref{sec:validator} reproduced the same failure on first contact with a
godet skirt, flagging 19 crossings on a perfectly sound pattern: a triangular
godet inserted into a skirt turns the hemline by roughly $51^\circ$ where it
enters, by design. The discriminating signature is that a corner between two
\emph{straight} edges is drawn, not accumulated --- a neckline is traced with
curves and is meant to flow, whereas a godet hem turns between two straight
lines because that is what was designed. The separation is clean on our
fixtures: in the reference t-shirt all six straight-to-straight crossings
deviate by exactly $0.00^\circ$, while in the godet skirt all 26 crossings are
straight-to-straight with a median deviation of $49^\circ$. Suppressing them
leaves the skirt silent and the t-shirt with two genuine warnings of
$29.2^\circ$ at the armhole, symmetric between left and right.

The general point is that a manufacturability checker is only as useful as its
precision. A checker that rejects everything conveys no information, cannot
gate a pipeline, and cannot serve as a reward signal. We consider the 143
suppressed dart tips a result of this work on equal footing with the 15
retained corners, and we report both because the literature on generative
patterns reports neither. That the same failure recurred, unprompted, when we
added a second family of checks suggests it is structural rather than
accidental: legitimate construction reliably looks defective to a geometric
rule stated without its exception.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Thresholds are uncalibrated.} The default limits are physically
plausible but have not been contrasted against real fabric by a professional
patternmaker. They are an explicit hypothesis, not a measurement, and the
absolute defect counts should be read accordingly. The relative claim --- that
accepted patterns contain defects the generator does not look for --- does not
depend on the exact thresholds.

\paragraph{Sample size and sampling distribution.} The audit covers 30 patterns
and the seam study 25. Moreover, random sampling over a parameter space is not
the distribution a text-conditioned model would produce; our numbers measure
how much of the space is invalid, not the hit rate of a trained generator.

\paragraph{No downstream demonstration.} We describe the validator's use as a
filter, a repair signal, a dataset sanitizer, and a training reward, but we do
not yet report a trained model that uses it. Establishing that filtering the
corpus improves a downstream generator is the natural next experiment and is
not claimed here.

\paragraph{Coverage and inference.} Levels 0 and 1 are implemented, with two
qualifications. Assembly checking reports the cost of sewing in the round
rather than true feasibility, which is a question of physical access and
belongs to Level 2 along with wearability. And continuity findings are a lower
bound whenever seam orientation is left undeclared, since the check credits the
pattern with whichever endpoint pairing suits it best; on existing corpora,
where no pattern declares \texttt{orient}, every continuity result in this paper
is of that conservative kind.

\paragraph{Single system studied.} All measurements are on GarmentCode, because
it was the only one of the three systems we could execute; ChatGarment and
AIpparel were analyzed by reading their code, as their weights and the
GarmentCodeData archive were unreachable from our environment and AIpparel
requires CUDA. Conclusions about their expressive ceilings are therefore
architectural, not empirical.

\section{Conclusion}

Generative garment systems are being evaluated on a property that does not
imply the one the industry needs. We measured the distance between the two:
77\% of patterns accepted by a widely used open generator carry defects it does
not check for, and a third of all seams contain a length disagreement whose
intent the format cannot express. We release a deterministic, CPU-only
validator for panel geometry and seam topology, three minimal format extensions
that make seam ease, edge finishing and seam orientation expressible, and the
benchmarks that produced these numbers.

We think the most immediate consequence is for training data rather than for
inference: a corpus of 115{,}000 synthetic garments produced by a generator
with this defect profile teaches those defects as correct. The second is
evaluative --- the fraction of manufacturable outputs is a comparable,
reproducible, GPU-free metric that the field currently does not report, and
which we believe it should.

available at \url{https://github.com/Camilo-neck/fashion-validator}.
